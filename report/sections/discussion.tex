\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the evidence supports}

The split is clean and it is about labels. \textbf{Given roughly 400
labelled examples for your task, a 1.7B model with an 87\,MB adapter beats
a commercial typed-decision API} on a hardened routing benchmark, runs on
your own hardware, returns full-precision probabilities, and is
deterministic. \textbf{Without labels, it is behind on every task we
measured}: across the seven held-out schemas Jev wins five and ties two,
$38.3\times$ chance against our $29.6\times$, with $0.863$ against
$0.605$ on Banking77 and $0.938$ against $0.702$ on the 151-way menu. We
compared on Banking77 alone for most of this work and called it a
twenty-one point gap; measuring the rest of the suite did not change the
direction of the result, only our confidence that it is general. On the
encoder Banking77 was $0.343$, so changing the backbone closed part of
that gap without new data and left the rest open.

Both halves matter. The first says the capability is not gated behind
scale or a vendor. The second says the vendor's actual product, working
on a schema you have never labelled, is a real and unsolved thing.

\subsection{When this fits a real problem}
\label{sec:when}

Published walkthroughs of the reference API give a useful external check
on whether our primitive set matches real demand. Three that circulated
widely: filtering 38{,}000 LinkedIn connections against an ideal-customer
profile, cleaning 4{,}710 auto-collected Gmail contacts, and triaging six
months of arXiv abstracts for relevance to non-technical readers.

All three use only \choice{}, \score{} and \noul{}; all three ask several
questions per record in one call; all three threshold on the returned
confidence. Menu sizes run from 4 to 15, well inside what we handle. So
the abstraction transfers, which is worth stating because we did not
design it and it could have turned out narrower than the real demand.

Whether \emph{our} implementation fits depends on one thing:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
 & no labels & a few hundred labels \\
\midrule
schema you have never seen & reference API & \openjev{} \\
cost per record at scale & comparable & \openjev{} (local) \\
need a real probability & \openjev{} & \openjev{} \\
need reproducibility & \openjev{} & \openjev{} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The probability and reproducibility rows are ours regardless of labels:
the reference quantises to a $0.01$ grid with $71.9\%$ of values at a hard
zero, which matters directly for the confidence thresholds all three of
those applications expose to the user, and it offers no seed.

Two practical observations. First, on our own evidence a task dominated by
\score{} questions is the worst case for \openjev{}, since the ordinal
primitive is where we only match a majority baseline; two of the three
walkthroughs contain such questions. Second, and more usefully: each of
those three workflows \emph{already} contains a labelling step, a
40-row corrected evaluation file in one and a 50-item checkpoint in
another, used for validation rather than training. The labels a
fine-tune needs are already being produced. Raising 40 to 300 converts a
validation artifact into a training set, and on our measurements that is
the difference between trailing the reference API and passing it.

\subsection{Which backbone to use}
\label{sec:when-backbone}

We ship both backbones, so the recommendation needs to be concrete rather
than a shrug. \textbf{Use the merged LoRA decoder unless one of two
specific constraints binds.}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
 & decoder, merged LoRA & encoder, fine-tuned & source \\
\midrule
held-out suite mean      & \textbf{29.6$\times$} & 17.2$\times$ & \S\ref{sec:res-heldout} \\
\texttt{clinc\_oos}, $K{=}151$ & \textbf{0.702} & 0.382 & \S\ref{sec:res-heldout} \\
Banking77 zero-shot      & \textbf{0.605} & 0.343 & \S\ref{sec:res-heldout} \\
router intent, fine-tuned & \textbf{0.979} & 0.899 & \S\ref{sec:res-router} \\
p95 per state, batch 1   & 56 ms & \textbf{20 ms} & \S\ref{sec:res-latency} \\
checkpoint on disk       & 3.4 GB & \textbf{0.6 GB} & \S\ref{sec:res-latency} \\
cost of a second task    & \textbf{87 MB adapter} & 0.6 GB checkpoint & \S\ref{sec:res-router} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Take the decoder by default.} It is better on all seven
held-out tasks, better by eight points of intent accuracy on the
fine-tuned router, and the merged adapter costs $1.07\times$ the
encoder's median latency, so the size difference mostly does not show up
in wall time at batch 1. Serving many schemas from one backbone is also
cheaper on this path, since each additional task is an $87$\,MB adapter
rather than another $0.6$\,GB of weights.

\paragraph{Take the encoder if the p95 tail is your budget.} The median
latencies are close; the tails are not, at $56$\,ms against $20$\,ms on
the router measurement. If your router sits on a request path with a hard
tail budget in the tens of milliseconds, that is the measurement that
decides it, and no amount of accuracy recovers a missed deadline. This is
the weakest recommendation in this subsection: our two latency runs
disagree on the size of the tail gap ($1.1\times$ under GPU contention
against $2.8\times$ under lighter load, \S\ref{sec:res-latency}), and we
did not resolve which is representative. Measure it on your own hardware
before letting it decide.

\paragraph{Take the encoder if 3.4\,GB does not fit.} A 0.6\,GB
checkpoint runs on hardware a 3.4\,GB one does not, including CPU-only
and small-GPU deployments we did not benchmark. This is a hard constraint
rather than a trade, and where it binds the encoder's $17.2\times$ is
still a working system.

\paragraph{Two things that should not enter the decision.} Do not leave
the adapter unmerged to keep the artifact separate at serving time: it
costs $1.96\times$ latency for output that is identical either way
(\S\ref{sec:res-latency}). And do not pick the backbone on held-out
benchmark transfer if your real task looks like our router, because that
ordering reverses (\S\ref{sec:res-deployment}). If you have labels,
fine-tune both and measure; it took 258 seconds on the decoder and 38 on
the encoder.

\subsection{Why the data mattered more than the model}

The most useful finding here is negative: a strong encoder with a clever
output format scores below chance untrained, and an untrained decoder with
the same format reaches only $11.5\times$ against the $29.6\times$ that
training produces. Nothing about scoring options at marker positions
confers generalization on either backbone. What produced it was the
composition of the training mixture, and in particular a property we could
manufacture, namely menu-size diversity, rather than one we had to find.
The backbone swap is worth $17.2\times$ to $29.6\times$ on top of that
data work, which is a large effect and still the second-largest one in
this paper.

This has a practical consequence. Our mixture is 234 \choice{} tasks
against 34 \noul{} and 11 \score{}, and the result pattern follows exactly:
\choice{} transfers, the other two barely do. That is a statement about
279 tasks scraped from one source, not about the architecture. The
fastest route to a better general model is a more balanced typed-decision
corpus, and assembling one is beyond what a single project can do.

\subsection{Limitations}

\paragraph{The ordinal primitive is weak.} \texttt{helpsteer} beats chance
but only draws level with predicting the most common label. The mixture
has 11 \score{} tasks, mostly three-level sentiment. We searched twice for
more; ordinal scales with five or more levels, in domains that are not
response quality, are scarce in accessible form. We deliberately excluded
LLM-response-rating corpora because they are the held-out task in all but
name, and training on a near-twin would move the number without moving the
ability.

\paragraph{No ordinal or rejection head.} \score{} is a softmax over
levels; we use the ordering only in rendering and in an expectation-based
readout. Rank-consistent objectives~\cite{corn} and adaptive rejection
boundaries~\cite{adb} are the obvious next step and are not implemented.

\paragraph{Zero-shot on a real task is much worse than on benchmarks, and
benchmark transfer does not rank models on it.} The decoder's
$29.6\times$ chance on the held-out suite coexists with intent $0.467$ on
the routing task, a multi-label exact set of $0.207$, and a pharmacy gate
at $0.440$ recall with a $0.696$ false-positive rate. Safety gates in
particular appear to need task supervision, and we would not deploy any
zero-shot gate on this evidence.

The stronger version of this caveat is the comparison with the encoder.
The decoder transfers far better on the benchmark suite ($29.6\times$
against $17.2\times$, every task improved) and yet is \emph{worse} than
the encoder zero-shot on the routing task, intent $0.467$ against
$0.601$. Its clinical-gate recall is the better of the two ($0.380$
against $0.111$) and still nowhere near usable, so this is a reversal
between two unusable systems rather than a case for the encoder.

The ordering that held across seven public benchmarks reverses on the
one task with an operational shape: multi-question packing, compound
multi-label answers, and gates with recall floors. We report this because
it undercuts the natural reading of our own headline result. Held-out
benchmark transfer measured what we asked it to measure and did not
predict deployment readiness, and we would not have discovered that by
running the suite alone. It does not change the architecture choice,
which rests on zero-shot transfer where no labels exist and on fine-tuned
accuracy where they do, and the decoder wins both; it does mean nobody
should read $29.6\times$ as a statement about a system anyone would ship
unsupervised.

\paragraph{Synthetic evaluation.} The routing task is handwritten and
systematically composed, not real traffic. It is evidence a method works,
not a claim about any production distribution.

\paragraph{One-day black-box baseline.} All reference figures are
measurements of a hosted endpoint on a single day from one network
location.

\paragraph{Untuned comparisons.} The LoRA-versus-full-fine-tune result
compares one hyperparameter setting each. We believe the direction and
not the magnitude.

\paragraph{Single runs, no seed variance.} Every number in this paper is
one training run. The confidence intervals are bootstrap over the test
set, which captures sampling noise but says nothing about run-to-run
variance from initialisation, data order, or augmentation sampling.
Differences of one or two points between configurations should not be
read as real on this evidence. The large effects (a literal $0.000$
becoming $0.205$; $0.666$ against $0.979$) are far outside any plausible
seed noise; the small ones are not.

\paragraph{The obvious baseline is missing.} We do not compare against
$m$ independent single-question classifiers, which is the natural
alternative to packing and the first thing a careful engineer would try.
Packing's advantage should be cost rather than accuracy, since separate
classifiers have strictly more capacity per question, and our claim is
about answering ten questions for the price of one. But we did not
measure it, so the accuracy side of that trade is unquantified here. Nor
do we compare against a general-purpose LLM with constrained decoding,
which is what most teams would reach for today.

\paragraph{Two tiers we expected to win are statistically level.} The
\texttt{compound\_3} tier and oblique clinical recall show large margins
($0.903$ vs $0.645$ and $0.966$ vs $0.793$) at $p = 0.057$ and $p =
0.063$, on 31 and 29 items. They are reported as ties. A larger version
of those tiers is the cheapest way to settle whether the margins are
real.

\subsection{Future work}

The experiment that would most change these conclusions is a balanced
typed-decision corpus with real \score{} and \noul{} diversity at the
scale Flan suggests. Beyond that: adapter composition, where a task
adapter is initialised from a general adapter, which is the
parameter-efficient analogue of the $+36$-point intermediate-task result;
rank-consistent ordinal heads; and calibrated rejection for out-of-scope
inputs.

\subsection{Conclusion}

Typed decisions do not need a large model, and they do not need
generation. They need the right output format, an attention mask that
keeps questions independent, and above all training data
whose \emph{shape} matches deployment. Of those three, only the last was
hard, and it is the one that transfers least from the literature to
practice.

The system we recommend is a 1.7B decoder with a rank-16 adapter merged
into it, which is small by current standards and large by the standards
of the encoder we started from. The encoder is not a failed branch: it
holds two deployment cases that the decoder does not, and three of the
findings above exist only because we trained both and compared them. The
version of this work with one backbone would have been shorter, cheaper,
and considerably less informative.
