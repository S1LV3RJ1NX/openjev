\subsection{Held-out transfer, and the backbone ablation it settles}
\label{sec:res-heldout}

Table~\ref{tab:heldout} is the central result of Study G: the LoRA decoder
trained on the audited 279-task mixture, evaluated on seven public tasks
it was never trained on, every task scored at its full advertised menu.
The encoder column is the same mixture, the same augmentation, the same
evaluation items, and the same harness, with only the backbone and its
readout changed. Harness sanity $1.000$; held-in control $0.847$ to
$0.980$ accuracy, $1.7$ to $2.0\times$ chance, on five tasks the model was
trained on, so a weak held-out number here would be a transfer result
rather than a bug.

Three things in the table matter beyond the mean. The high-cardinality
end is where the gap is widest: a 151-way menu at $0.702$ against
$0.382$, and a 77-way at $0.605$ against $0.343$. \textbf{Every task
beats its majority-class baseline}, not merely $1/K$, which the encoder
did not manage: \texttt{civil\_comments} and \texttt{sst5} clear theirs at
$p<10^{-15}$, and \texttt{helpsteer} reaches $0.273$ against $0.233$ at
$p = 0.010$, a margin thin enough that a change in sampling could erase
it. On the binary task we also report ranking rather than argmax alone,
where \texttt{civil\_comments} reaches AUROC $0.828$.

\textbf{Banking77 zero-shot rises from $0.343$ to $0.605$} against the
commercial reference's $0.863$. That closes part of the gap and leaves
most of it. It is not parity and we do not claim it;
Section~\ref{sec:res-jev-zeroshot} measures the same comparison on all
seven tasks.

\paragraph{These numbers replace an earlier, inflated set.} Auditing our
own results against a clean clone of the public repository exposed a
measurement fault. The packer drops options to fit \texttt{max\_len},
always retaining the gold option, and our evaluation runs passed
\texttt{--max-len 2048}. At that budget \texttt{clinc\_oos} presented
64 of its 151 options and \texttt{banking77} presented 64 of 77, while
the multiple of chance was still computed against $1/151$ and $1/77$. The
accuracies were real; the multiples credited the model for a discrimination
it never performed. We previously reported $32.8\times$ for the decoder
and $21.9\times$ for the encoder; the corrected figures are
$29.6\times$ and $17.2\times$, Banking77 falls from $0.737$ to $0.605$,
and \texttt{clinc\_oos} from $0.783$ to $0.702$. Correcting it took two
attempts: at $4096$ tokens banking77's 77 options fit but
\texttt{clinc\_oos} is still clipped to 64, because the decoder's
per-option template costs tokens the encoder's does not, and the full
151-way menu needs $6144$. The encoder figure additionally came from an
in-training evaluator on a superseded version of the suite and did not
reproduce. \texttt{eval\_heldout.py} now records the menu the model
actually saw, scores against that, prints $K\rightarrow k$ when they
differ, and names every truncated task in a closing warning.

\paragraph{The architecture decision.} Our ablation registry fixed three
possible outcomes before this run so that the choice could not be
rationalised afterwards. Latency does not separate the backbones once the
adapter is merged ($1.07\times$, \S\ref{sec:res-latency}), and a
1.7B model fits a consumer GPU, so zero-shot transfer was the deciding
axis. The decoder takes it on all seven tasks and on the mean,
$17.2\times$ to $29.6\times$. \textbf{We therefore make the merged LoRA
decoder the default artifact}, and retain the encoder as the documented
alternative for the two deployments where it still wins: a p95 latency
budget it meets at $20$\,ms and the decoder does not at $56$\,ms, and a
$0.6$\,GB footprint against $3.4$\,GB. \S\ref{sec:when-backbone} states
that choice as a rule. The encoder's own held-out results, and the
findings that only its presence made visible, are in
\S\ref{sec:abl-encoder} and \S\ref{sec:abl-sd6}.

Widening the comparison did not change its direction, only our confidence
that the direction is general. It also moved their Banking77 number from
$0.820$ to $0.863$; the service is neither deterministic nor static, so
both are measurements at a point in time rather than properties of the
product.

This is the sharpest statement of what this paper does and does not
claim. \textbf{An open 1.7B model with 87\,MB of adapters does not
approach a commercial typed-decision API zero-shot, on any task we
tried.} The case for building one rests on the other half of the result:
a few hundred labels of your own task reverse the ordering, which
Section~\ref{sec:res-router} quantifies.

\subsection{The output format makes zero-shot scoring possible, not
present}
\label{sec:res-architecture}

Before any task training, we score the held-out suite using only the
output format and a pretrained backbone.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llr}
\toprule
backbone & configuration & held-out mean \\
\midrule
decoder, 1.7B   & state and options concatenated plainly  & 0.8$\times$ chance \\
decoder, 1.7B   & \quad + each option framed as yes/no    & 2.9$\times$ chance \\
decoder, 1.7B   & \quad + a preamble stating the task     & \textbf{11.5$\times$ chance} \\
decoder, 1.7B   & \quad \emph{control:} shortened framing & 5.7$\times$ chance \\
\midrule
encoder, 150M   & pretrained masked-LM head at the marker & 0.7$\times$ chance \\
encoder, 395M   & same readout, larger model              & 2.2$\times$ chance \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The two halves of the table are different backbones and are not directly
comparable; each is internally consistent. Within the decoder rows, the
format is worth an order of magnitude, and the last decoder row is the
control that matters: shortening the framing to
\texttt{\{opt\}\textbackslash ncorrect? answer} roughly halves the mean,
so the gain is the format doing work rather than an incidental wording
choice.

Within the encoder rows, a strong bidirectional model with the same
output format scores \emph{below chance}, and its larger sibling barely
clears it. This is the negative result that organises the rest of the
paper. Nothing about scoring options at marker positions confers
generalization, on either backbone: $11.5\times$ from prompt framing
alone is a long way from the $29.6\times$ that training produces, and
$0.7\times$ is worse than guessing. What the format buys is a well-formed
output and a cheap one, not an ability.

The same sensitivity appears in option rendering. On the held-out binary
task, scoring the two \noul{} descriptions rather than bare polarity costs
AUROC $0.63$ against $0.712$, because the descriptions restate one
judgement from opposite sides and a model scoring both measures their
overlap.

\subsection{What produced the transfer}
\label{sec:res-data}

Label-space augmentation is the single largest effect we measured:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
held-out task & $K$ & before & after \\
\midrule
\texttt{banking77}       &  77 & 0.000 & \textbf{0.205} \\
\texttt{clinc\_oos}      & 151 & 0.000 & \textbf{0.180} \\
\texttt{massive\_intent} &  60 & 0.040 & \textbf{0.305} \\
\texttt{ag\_news}        &   4 & 0.225 & 0.655 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Two exact zeroes become usable numbers. You do not need datasets with
large menus; you can synthesize them. The caveat is that the mixture also
grew from 61 to 141 tasks in the same change, so scale and shape are not
separated in this pair.

Scaling the audited mixture to 279 tasks took the mean from $17.6\times$
to $17.2\times$ and moved every task (Figure~\ref{fig:transfer}).

Both of those measurements were made on the encoder, before the backbone
was settled. We did not repeat the augmentation ablation on the decoder,
so the effect size is an encoder effect size; the decoder was trained on
the post-augmentation mixture throughout and we cannot say from this work
how much of its $29.6\times$ the augmentation is responsible for.

\subsection{Router: a fine-tuned small model against a zero-shot API}
\label{sec:res-router}

\paragraph{The comparison is asymmetric, in our favour, and we state it
before the numbers.} \openjev{} is fine-tuned on 395 labelled examples of
this task. The reference API is not: it has never seen this task's
training split, and produces its answers from the schema alone. This is
not a like-for-like contest, and a reader should discount it accordingly.
What it establishes is narrower than ``we beat Jev'': \emph{if you can
label a few hundred examples, a 1.7B open model reaches and passes a
closed API operating zero-shot on the same task.} That is a claim about
the value of labels as much as about either model.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrrl}
\toprule

\midrule

multi-label exact set         & \textbf{0.909} & 0.822 & 57 & 18 & 7.2e-06 & \openjev{} \\
pharmacy scope gate           & \textbf{0.978} & 0.880 & 49 &  5 & 3.9e-10 & \openjev{} \\

\paragraph{Two tier diagnostics that look like wins are not.} Below the
primary measures, two tiers show large margins and fail the significance
test:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrrl}
\toprule
tier & \openjev{} & reference & $b_{10}$ & $b_{01}$ & $p$ & $n$ \\
\midrule
\texttt{compound\_3} exact set & 0.903 & 0.645 & 11 & 3 & 0.057 & 31 \\
oblique clinical recall        & 0.966 & 0.793 &  5 & 0 & 0.063 & 29 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

We report both as level, because that is what the test says. They are the
two tiers we most expected to win, which is exactly why they need the test
rather than the margin, and at 31 and 29 items the oblique-clinical tier
floors at $p = 0.0625$ even with zero losses. Read them as underpowered
rather than as parity.

\texttt{compound\_3} is nonetheless where the \noul{} primitive earns its
place in design terms: three simultaneous intents in one message, which a
single \choice{} softmax cannot express at all, whatever the $p$-value on
31 items says.

\paragraph{The smallest configuration is the best one.}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
configuration & intent & trainable & artifact \\
\midrule
\textbf{decoder, LoRA $r{=}16$} & \textbf{0.979} & \textbf{17M} & \textbf{87 MB} \\
decoder, all open            & 0.929 & 1{,}725M & 3.4 GB \\
decoder, head only           & 0.666 & 4.2M & 17 MB \\
encoder, all open            & 0.899 & 150M & 0.6 GB \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Adapting 17M parameters beats opening 1{,}725M, at $1/39$ the artifact.
The likely explanation is the learning rate a full fine-tune tolerates:
$10^{-5}$ over six epochs of 395 examples barely moves a 1.7B model, while
LoRA at $2\times10^{-4}$ adapts quickly without disturbing the weights it
rides on. Neither was tuned beyond one setting, so this is ``adapters are
the right default here'' rather than a tuned optimum. The encoder row is
the alternative backbone fully fine-tuned, which is the strongest thing we
could do with it on this task, and it lands eight points below the
adapter.

\paragraph{Intermediate-task transfer, which works on one backbone only.}
Starting the \emph{encoder} specialist from a general checkpoint rather
than raw weights, with identical data and schedule, moves intent
$0.544 \to 0.899$ and multi-label $0.453 \to 0.789$
($p = 6\mathrm{e}{-18}$). Two safety gates that never fired at all from
scratch reach $0.857$ and $0.583$ recall. This is the small-target-data
regime where STILTs~\cite{stilts} predicts the largest gain, and it is one
of the strongest single interventions in this work.

\paragraph{The same intervention hurts the decoder.} The $0.979$ LoRA
used no mixture training at all; it started from base Qwen3-1.7B, and
starting it from our general adapter instead is significantly
\emph{worse} ($0.953$, $p = 0.012$). \S\ref{sec:abl-sd6} works through
why. The general checkpoint earns its keep on schemas you have no labels
for, not on the task you actually care about.

\subsection{Throughput under concurrent load}
\label{sec:res-throughput}

Every latency figure in this paper is batch size 1, the interactive case,
and the reciprocal of a single-request latency is not a throughput
number. Table~\ref{tab:throughput} sweeps batch size as a stand-in for
in-flight requests, which is what a server batching arrivals sees.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
 & \multicolumn{2}{c}{encoder} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{decoder} \\
batch & states/s & p50 & states/s & p50 \\
\midrule
 1 &  47.4 & 20.4\,ms & 23.2 &  42.9\,ms \\
 4 & 188.7 & 20.8\,ms & 74.5 &  54.7\,ms \\
 8 & 373.9 & 21.0\,ms & 77.1 & 105.0\,ms \\
16 & \textbf{654.6} & 24.0\,ms & 77.3 & 206.4\,ms \\
32 & 532.7 & 36.8\,ms & \textbf{84.4} & 417.0\,ms \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Sustained throughput, ten questions per state, idle H100, one
process. Each state answers ten questions, so the encoder's peak is
6{,}546 decisions per second against the decoder's 844.}
\label{tab:throughput}
\end{table}

The reference API peaks near 47 requests per second at 32 in-flight, with
its median flat around $436$\,ms, and degrades past that: at 64 concurrent
its median reaches $1{,}106$\,ms while throughput falls to $29.8$/s. On
that comparison the decoder sustains $84.4$/s at a comparable $417$\,ms,
and the encoder sustains $654.6$/s at $24$\,ms.

Two caveats, both material. Ours is a single process on one GPU with no
HTTP layer, no queuing and no network, while theirs is a hosted service
measured across the internet from India, so this bounds what the hardware
can do rather than what a productionised OpenJev would serve. And the
encoder has a knee of its own between batch 16 and 32, where throughput
falls from $654.6$ to $532.7$ and p95 rises from $29.4$ to $209.1$\,ms,
the same character as the reference system's knee at a different
position.

\paragraph{This is the strongest case for the encoder in this paper.} It
was already the choice when p95 latency binds. It also serves roughly
eight times the decoder's throughput, and that gap is larger than the
accuracy gap running the other way. A deployment answering millions of
decisions a day should read Table~\ref{tab:throughput} before
Table~\ref{tab:heldout}.

\subsection{Latency and footprint}
\label{sec:res-latency}

Two measurements, taken under different load, so the absolute values are
not comparable across them. The first is a back-to-back comparison of the
three serving configurations while a training run shared the GPU, which
inflates every row equally and leaves the ratios readable:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
 & p50 & p95 & vs encoder \\
\midrule
encoder, 150M             & 37.7 ms & 50.9 ms & baseline \\
decoder LoRA, unmerged    & 74.0 ms & 110.5 ms & 1.96$\times$ \\
decoder LoRA, merged      & \textbf{40.4 ms} & \textbf{55.9 ms} & \textbf{1.07$\times$} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{An unmerged adapter costs roughly $2\times$ for nothing}, since it
is an extra matmul per target module at every layer and the output is
identical either way. Merged, a model eleven times larger runs 7\% slower.
The released loader therefore merges on construction
(\S\ref{sec:method-lora}), so this cost cannot be paid by accident.

The second measurement is the fine-tuned router at batch size 1, ten
questions and twenty-four options per state, under lighter load. It is the
one the deployment recommendation rests on, because the tail is what a
latency budget is written against:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
 & decoder, 1{,}725M & encoder, 150M \\
\midrule
p95 per state & 56 ms & \textbf{20 ms} \\
checkpoint on disk & 3.4 GB & \textbf{0.6 GB} \\
adapter, per additional task & \textbf{87 MB} & 0.6 GB \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The tail, not the median, is where the two backbones separate, and it is
the only axis on which the encoder is clearly ahead.

\paragraph{The two measurements disagree about how much, and we have not
settled it.} Under GPU contention the merged decoder's p95 is
$1.1\times$ the encoder's ($55.9$ against $50.9$\,ms); on the lighter-load
router it is closer to $2.8\times$ ($56$ against $20$\,ms). The likely
reading is that contention inflates the encoder's tail far more than the
decoder's in relative terms, since both are sitting on the same queue, so
the lighter-load figures are the ones a dedicated deployment would see.
We did not re-run the merge comparison on an idle GPU to confirm that, so
anyone with a tail budget in the tens of milliseconds should measure on
their own hardware rather than take either ratio from us. Everything here
is batch size 1, which is the interactive case; sustained throughput under
load is not measured in this work.

\subsection{Benchmark transfer is not deployment readiness}
\label{sec:res-deployment}

The general decoder scores $29.6\times$ chance on the held-out suite and
the general encoder $17.2\times$. Applied zero-shot to the routing task,
that ordering reverses:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
 & decoder & encoder & reference \\
 & \emph{zero-shot} & \emph{zero-shot} & \emph{zero-shot} \\
\midrule
intent               & 0.467 & 0.601 & \textbf{0.941} \\
clinical gate recall & 0.380 & 0.111 & \textbf{0.926} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The decoder wins all seven public benchmarks and is worse than the encoder
on intent for the one task with an operational shape: multi-question
packing, compound multi-label answers, and gates with recall floors.
Neither backbone is usable there. Intent at $0.601$ on a six-way menu is
genuinely above chance, so some transfer is real, but a clinical gate
firing on one in nine of the cases it should catch is not a gate, and
$0.380$ is not one either. The decoder's other zero-shot figures on this
task are no better: multi-label exact set $0.207$, and a pharmacy scope
gate at $0.440$ recall with a $0.696$ false-positive rate. Fine-tuning on
395 examples of the task takes the same decoder to intent $0.979$ and
multi-label exact set $0.909$ (Table~\ref{tab:router}), so what is missing
zero-shot is task supervision rather than capacity.

We report this because it undercuts the natural reading of our own
headline result. It does not change the architecture decision, which rests
on zero-shot transfer where no labels exist and on fine-tuned accuracy
where they do, and the decoder wins both of those. It does mean that
clearing chance on academic benchmarks with clean label sets is a
materially weaker claim than working on a deployment task you have no
labels for, that the two come apart sharply here, and that nobody should
read $29.6\times$ as a statement about a system anyone would ship
unsupervised. Safety gates in particular appear to require task
supervision.
