Many production decisions are not open-ended generation but a fixed menu:
route this message to one of 77 intents, flag whether it mentions clinical
risk, rate its urgency. Jev, a commercial API from TypeSafe, makes exactly
this the primitive, and does it well: it answers many typed questions about
one input in a single call, and on a 77-way intent task it has never seen
it scores roughly $0.820$. This paper asks what it takes to build an open
equivalent, and reports honestly where that succeeds and where it does not.

\paragraph{What we built.} \openjev{} encodes the state once as a shared
prefix, places every candidate option after it behind a marker token,
isolates questions from one another with block-diagonal attention, and
normalises scores within each question's own option set. Ten questions
therefore cost one forward pass and 6\% more latency than one, and the
output is a calibrated probability over a closed menu rather than generated
text. The system we recommend, and the one every headline number here
refers to, is rank-16 LoRA adapters on a 1.7B decoder backbone, merged
into the frozen weights at load. A 150M bidirectional encoder runs the
identical format and is retained as the documented alternative and as the
control in our architecture ablation. We release the code, two adapters,
two encoder checkpoints, and three datasets including a 279-task,
323{,}466-row training mixture.

\paragraph{Held-out transfer.} On seven public tasks never trained on,
600 rows each, every task scored at its full advertised menu, the LoRA
decoder reaches \textbf{29.6$\times$ chance}: a 151-way menu at $0.702$
($106.0\times$), a 60-way at $0.775$, a 77-way at $0.605$, and every task
clearing both $1/K$ and its majority-class baseline. Trained on identical
data and scored on identical items, the encoder reaches
\textbf{17.2$\times$}, loses on all seven tasks, and leaves the ordinal
one level with its majority baseline. That ablation settles the
architecture, since merging the adapter removes the latency argument for
the encoder ($1.07\times$).

\paragraph{The main finding is negative, and it is the useful one.} The
architecture does not generalize on its own. A strong bidirectional encoder
with this exact output format, untrained, scores \textbf{0.7$\times$
chance} on held-out schemas, and a 61-task training mixture leaves it at
0.9$\times$. Generalization is instead a property of the training data.
Padding training menus with borrowed distractor labels, together with a
mixture rebuild in the same change, moves a 77-way held-out menu from a
literal $0.000$ to $0.205$; a model that has never seen a large menu
collapses onto one label when handed one, though we did not separate the
two interventions and so cannot attribute the gain cleanly. The backbone
swap moves the suite from $17.2\times$ to $29.6\times$; the data work is
the difference between nothing and a working system on either backbone.

\paragraph{Both headline figures are corrections.} Auditing our own results
against a clean clone found that at the context budget we had been using,
the packer silently dropped a 151-option menu to 64 while the multiple of
chance was still computed against $1/151$. The decoder's $32.8\times$
became $29.6\times$ and the encoder's $21.9\times$ became $17.2\times$,
Banking77 fell from $0.737$ to $0.605$, and \texttt{clinc\_oos} fell from
$0.783$ to $0.702$. The encoder figure additionally came from an
in-training evaluator on a superseded version of the suite and did not
reproduce. We report the withdrawn numbers alongside the corrected ones.

\paragraph{Against Jev, the result splits cleanly on one axis: labels.}
Given 395 labelled examples and 258 seconds, a rank-16 LoRA adapter on
Qwen3-1.7B scores intent $0.979$ against Jev's $0.941$
($p=9.8\mathrm{e}{-04}$) and multi-label exact set $0.909$ against $0.822$
($p=7.2\mathrm{e}{-6}$) on a hardened healthcare routing benchmark: three
wins, four statistical ties, no losses. \textbf{The comparison is
asymmetric and in our favour}, since we are fine-tuned on that task and
Jev is zero-shot on it, so the claim it supports is that a few hundred
labels are worth more than the gap between an open 1.7B model and a
closed API. The adapter is $87$\,MB and outperforms opening all
$1{,}725$M parameters ($0.929$, $3.4$\,GB). Curiously, initialising that
adapter from our general checkpoint rather than from base weights makes
it \emph{worse} ($0.953$, $p=0.012$), reversing a $+36$ point gain from
the same intervention on the encoder: staged fine-tuning pays only when
the base model cannot perform the task form at all. Without labels the
picture inverts: $0.605$ on Banking77 against Jev's $0.820$, twenty-one
points behind, and applied zero-shot to the routing task the decoder
scores intent $0.467$ against the encoder's $0.601$, so the benchmark
ordering does not survive contact with a deployment task.

\paragraph{Takeaway.} Use Jev, or something like it, when you need typed
decisions on a schema you have no labels for; that capability is real and
we did not reproduce it. Use \openjev{} when you can label a few hundred
examples of your actual task, want the model on your own hardware, need
full-precision probabilities and determinism, or need multi-label routing
where a single choice cannot express the answer. Within \openjev{}, take
the merged LoRA decoder unless a p95 latency budget near $20$\,ms or a
$0.6$\,GB footprint rules it out, in which case the encoder is the
documented fallback at a measured cost in accuracy. Benchmark transfer and
deployment readiness are not the same thing, and we report the gap between
them rather than the better number. All claims are reproducible from a
public repository, with negative and retracted results given the same
prominence as the positive ones.
